# How Traders Can Work on Profitability: Seven Habits Worth Checking

A trading rule can survive a backtest and disappear after two losses.

That gap deserves attention. Strategy quality, execution and trading costs all affect results. Mix them together and every disappointing week looks like a reason to buy another indicator.

**To work on trading profitability, separate strategy expectancy, execution discipline and costs. Record what the plan required, what happened and what each trade cost. Investigate one repeatable leak at a time.**

## What the evidence tells us

[Barber and Odean](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers%20current%20versions/individual_investor_performance_final.pdf) studied 66,465 US brokerage households during 1991–1996. The most active earned 11.4% annually after trading costs, compared with 17.9% for the market. This historical sample raises a useful question: what is extra activity costing?

In [Chague, De-Losso and Giovannetti's study](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:fgv:eesptd:525), 97% of individuals who started day trading Brazilian equity futures during 2013–2015 and persisted for at least 300 days lost money. The authors found no learning through day trading in that sample.

Neither study tested the routine below. They challenge the assumption that repetition alone produces improvement.

## Expectancy makes win rate less misleading

Let R be the amount initially risked on a trade. A simplified model is:

**Expectancy = win rate × average win in R − loss rate × average loss in R − average costs in R.**

These figures are arithmetic illustrations, not observed returns:

| Illustration | Win rate | Average win | Average loss | Expectancy before costs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 60% | 1R | 1.5R | 0.00R |
| B | 40% | 2.5R | 1R | +0.40R |
| B with larger average losses | 40% | 2.5R | 1.5R | +0.10R |

The last row loses three-quarters of the model's pre-cost expectancy. Add 0.10R of average costs and the remainder becomes zero. A real change in stop placement can also change win rate and average wins; the table holds those constant to show the arithmetic.

## Seven habits worth checking

### 1. Turn a vague rule into an if–then decision

“Be patient” is a mood. “If my entry conditions are incomplete, I wait and record the missing condition” is checkable.

[Gollwitzer and Sheeran](https://cancercontrol.cancer.gov/sites/default/files/2020-06/goal_intent_attain.pdf) summarise 94 studies showing improved goal attainment from if–then plans. They concern other behaviours, not trading returns. Apply the mechanism: write three to five rules with a visible trigger and a specific response.

### 2. Preserve the original reasoning

After the close, the chart is an excellent storyteller. It is less reliable as a record of what you thought beforehand.

Record your expectation, confidence and evidence before the session. Preserve that version and add a separate review afterward. A directional practice call evaluates that prediction task; strategy returns require their own record.

### 3. Define risk before the uncomfortable moment

Decide how position size, invalidation and session stopping conditions will be determined. Choose limits suited to the instrument, account and circumstances.

Review intended risk against actual loss, costs and slippage. Stops can fill beyond their trigger price, and several positions can share the same underlying risk. The useful comparison is planned process versus actual execution.

### 4. Let “no qualifying setup” count

Record why a session qualified for your setup or did not. That provides a denominator beyond the trades you happened to take.

Track trade count and costs together. Fewer trades are not automatically better; ask whether additional trades contribute after costs and within the plan. An uneventful session can still complete the practice. Very inconsiderate of the adrenaline budget, but useful for the record.

### 5. Separate execution from the outcome

Ask two questions: did the trade follow the rules, and what was its result?

| Result | Followed the rules | Broke a rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Won | Record execution; keep testing the strategy | Record the deviation; profit does not validate it |
| Lost | Compare the loss with expected risk | Investigate the deviation and its contribution |

**Rule adherence tests execution. Outcomes help test whether the strategy deserves to continue. Neither replaces the other.**

A discipline rate can track followed applicable checks divided by all applicable recorded checks. Keep that definition stable. Differences between high- and low-discipline sessions suggest questions; small observational samples do not establish causation.

### 6. Put scheduled events in the plan

Check official calendars for [CPI](https://www.bls.gov/schedule/news_release/cpi.htm), [employment reports](https://www.bls.gov/schedule/news_release/empsit.htm) and [Federal Reserve meetings](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm).

Record the time, scenario and your participation rule. Data, expectations, positioning and context can all influence the response. “Good number means up” is too small a model for a complicated market.

### 7. Make the routine repeatable

[Lally and colleagues](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/ejsp.674) studied everyday habit formation: repetition in a consistent context helped automaticity, with wide variation in timing. Missing one opportunity did not materially derail the process. It was not a trading study.

Attach the smallest useful review to a stable cue. A streak can remind you to practise; it is not a reason to invent a trade.

## A 15-minute daily routine

| When | Time | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Before the session | 3 minutes | Check scheduled events |
| Before the session | 2 minutes | Record the original call and evidence |
| Before the session | 5 minutes | Read rules and risk decisions |
| After the session | 5 minutes | Review outcome and one execution deviation |

Recording checks during the session and importing trades take additional time. Use a separate weekly review to examine costs, sample size and one rule worth testing.
